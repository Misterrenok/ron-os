## 5. Behavioral hypotheses to test — not facts

H1. **Transition bottleneck:** the largest gap may occur between intention/instruction and the first physical action.
H2. **Attention capture:** a desirable task may still be missed if attention remains captured by another activity during the transition window.
H3. **Instruction overload:** instructions that are too large, multi-stage or context-blind may reduce execution quality.
H4. **Just-in-time specificity:** one concrete physical action at a time may outperform broad plans.
H5. **Real-world friction matters:** missing food/equipment, travel, class availability, fatigue, hunger, queues, weather or interruptions can dominate an otherwise good plan.
H6. **External structure may help:** real appointments/social/operational consequences may improve transition reliability, but this must be observed rather than assumed.
H7. **Overcontrol cost:** excessive assistant micromanagement may itself create friction or weaken autonomy; today should test this rather than presume total delegation is superior.

## 6. Error taxonomy for today's analysis

When something goes wrong, classify only after enough evidence:
- MISSED_TRANSITION — intended action never physically started;
- ATTENTION_CAPTURE — time passed unnoticed while engaged elsewhere;
- OVERLOAD — instruction too large/complex;
- RESOURCE_GAP — required item/tool/access unavailable;
- SCHEDULE_CONFLICT — two legitimate commitments compete;
- PHYSICAL_STATE — fatigue, illness, pain, hunger or similar materially limits execution;
- ENVIRONMENT — transport, queue, noise, people, closure, connectivity, etc.;
- INSTRUCTION_DEFECT — assistant omitted/misread a material constraint;
- DELIBERATE_DEFERRAL — Ron consciously chose not to start despite feasible context;
- START_THEN_DROPOUT — action began but was not sustained;
- EXECUTION_VARIANCE — completed differently from instruction;
- UNKNOWN_CAUSE — insufficient evidence; do not force a story.

Multiple labels may apply.
